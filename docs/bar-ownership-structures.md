# Pattaya bar ownership structures — how they work and why

**Anonymised handoff note. No venues, companies, groups or individuals are named.**

Derived from registry work on one Pattaya bar street (~95 operating venues) set against a
province-wide control population of **570 companies** registered under the Thai objective code for
on-premises alcoholic beverage service, plus a 1,393-POI nightlife inventory for the wider city.
Confidence is marked throughout. The central finding is that **almost nothing about these
structures is about concealment; nearly all of it is about immigration law.**

---

## 1. The headline: most venues have no corporate existence at all

**CONFIRMED.** On the studied street, ~95 operating venues correspond to **24** bar companies
registered at street addresses. City-wide the ratio is worse: matching 1,393 nightlife POIs against
all 570 sector companies by the companies' *own registered English names* yields roughly **18
genuine matches — about 1.3%**, i.e. one venue in seventy-five.

That test was deliberately generous — a venue counted as matched if its company existed **anywhere
in the province**, not just at the venue address. Even so, ~98.7% of venues have no matchable bar
company.

**This is not evasion. It is the absence of a reason to incorporate.** A bar below the VAT
threshold, employing only Thais, has no legal need for a company and no commercial benefit from one.
Incorporation is the exception in this sector, not the rule, and its absence is not evidence of
anything.

**Practical consequence:** a registry-first methodology will see roughly a quarter of one street and
almost none of the city. Absence from the register is the normal state and must never be reported as
a finding in itself.

---

## 2. When a company *does* exist, it usually exists to carry a foreigner

**CONFIRMED at population level.** Registered capital in this sector does not track business size —
it tracks the work-permit ladder.

| Capital | Meaning under the permit rules | Share of 570 |
|---|---|---|
| 1,000,000 | one permit for a foreigner **married to a Thai** | 16.7% |
| 2,000,000 | one standard permit | **41.8%** |
| 3,000,000 | *buys nothing extra* | **2.8%** |
| 4,000,000 | two standard permits | 18.4% |
| 5,000,000 | two standard + one spouse-rule | 8.6% |

**88.2% of the entire population sits exactly on 1/2/3/4/5m.** The rules: **THB 2m of registered
capital per foreign work permit, halved to 1m where the employee is married to a Thai national**,
with an accompanying Thai-staff ratio. Median capital is 2m — the modal bar company is a
**one-permit company**.

**The proof is the hole at 3m, not the peaks.** If capital were chosen for business scale with a
preference for round numbers, frequency would fall monotonically as the number rises. Instead 3m is
**six and a half times rarer than 4m** despite being smaller, rounder and cheaper. The distribution
has a gap precisely where the permit ladder has no rung.

**Corollary — capital below the grid.** Companies under 1m (2.3%, and only ~1% of those registered
since 2020) signal that **nobody is being sponsored**: either there is no foreigner, or the
foreigner's right to remain comes from elsewhere (retirement, elite/privilege, LTR, marriage), or
they are passive or non-resident — or they are working unpermitted. **These readings are
observationally identical** without a permit record.

---

## 3. The legal-form ladder, and what each rung can do

This is the single most useful frame for the sector.

| Form | Capital | Accounts | **Can sponsor a work permit?** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Limited company | yes, on the grid | audited, filed | **Yes** |
| Registered sole proprietorship | none | none | **No** |
| Nothing at all | — | — | **No** |

**A venue with no registration cannot lawfully employ a foreigner** — not "chooses not to",
*cannot*, because the sponsorship test is a capital test and there is no capital. Where ~70% of a
street is unregistered, it follows structurally that any foreigner working in those venues is
working unpermitted. **This is a property of the arrangement, not an allegation about any person.**

**Partnerships behave differently and this confirms the frame.** Within the same province, industry
and objective code, partnerships show 4m capital at **3.1%** against limited companies' **19.3%**
(one observed against 6.2 expected; binomial p ≈ 0.009), are far less grid-bound (69% vs 89%) and
sit a full rung lower (median 1m vs 2m). The reason is structural: the standard foreign-participation
arrangement needs **shares** to split, and a partnership has none. **The capital grid belongs to the
share-issuing form** — the one built for foreign participation.

---

## 4. Two foreign-ownership patterns, chosen by personal circumstance

**CONFIRMED (n=2 for the open pattern — treat as illustrative, not a norm).**

**Pattern A — open foreign ownership.** The foreigner takes equity directly at **exactly 51/49**, the
foreign-ownership ceiling, and also sits as a director. Observed twice with two different
nationalities and capital differing by a factor of twenty — **and the same 51.00/49.00 split to the
share in both.** Not 50/50, not 60/40. A split chosen by a rule, not negotiated between parties.

**Pattern B — the spouse structure.** The foreign participant holds **zero equity**. The Thai spouse
holds the company outright; the foreigner participates as an **employee on a work permit** at the
reduced 1m rate. This is the ordinary consequence of foreign-ownership law plus the spouse discount,
not a concealment device.

**The critical point for investigators: 100% Thai ownership is not a red flag in this sector.** When
a foreigner owns a bar company here, **it shows on the register, at 49%, plainly, with the foreigner
also named as a director.** The visible route is available, cheap, legal and used. A structure
showing no foreign name is better explained by the spouse route than by hidden ownership — and a
zero-equity foreign operator is the *expected* shape where the foreigner is married locally.

**Corroborating observation (not from this sector):** where the same kind of operator incorporates in
a jurisdiction with no such constraint, they take and openly declare equity. Behaviour differs by
legal environment rather than by disposition.

---

## 5. Compliance tracks permits, not profitability

**SUPPORTED, with a confound I could not eliminate.**

| Capital | Still active | Struck off | **Filed accounts** |
|---|---|---|---|
| ≤1m | 40% | 46% | 38% |
| 2m | 47% | 44% | **43%** |
| 4m | 59% | 28% | **68%** |
| >5m | 68% | 21% | 85% |

The 4m cohort is **half as likely to be struck off** and files at 68% against 43%. **The gap holds
inside every registration era** (pre-2016: 71% vs 39%; 2016–20: 67% vs 35%; 2021+: 64% vs 52%), so it
is not an age artefact.

**The mechanism:** a company carrying foreign permits has staff whose *permission to remain in the
country* depends annually on the company being in good standing. That is a far stronger incentive
than the ordinary, widely-ignored filing duty. Non-compliance costs a person their visa rather than
costing the company a fine.

**The confound:** capital may simply proxy size, and bigger businesses file more regardless. This
cannot be controlled for, because revenue is only observable *for companies that filed* — the
non-filers are precisely those with no revenue figure, so any revenue-matched comparison is
selection-biased by construction. Two facts sit awkwardly with a pure size story: the revenue gap
(~1.4×) is smaller than the filing and survival gaps, and 3m companies — **not a permit rung** —
behave like 4m ones on both measures, which size predicts and permits do not.

---

## 6. Mortality: how these companies die, and which death means something

**CONFIRMED.** Roughly **half the sector is dead**.

- **still operating — 52%**
- **struck off for non-filing — 36%** ← the default
- **liquidation completed — under 10%**
- dissolved — 2%

Being struck off is **free and passive**: you simply stop. Formal liquidation **costs money**, needs
a shareholders' resolution, an auditor and filings.

**Therefore death is not the signal — the *route* is.** A company that was deliberately and promptly
wound up, especially one that never traded, is a purpose-built object whose purpose ended. That is
rare and worth attention. A company that was struck off is one of the 36% and worth none.

---

## 7. Venue layer and company layer are different things

**CONFIRMED, and this is the most common source of error.**

- **Venues rebrand; companies do not follow.** A venue can trade under one name for years, rebrand,
  and have an entirely separate company incorporated at the changeover — or none.
- **A venue can outlive its company by years.** Observed: a company wound up two months after the
  venue it was named for opened, with the venue still trading years later.
- **A registered address is not the premises.** Companies register at accountants' offices, serviced
  offices, home addresses and other districts entirely. A company named for a venue on one street may
  be registered 40km away.
- **Registered objectives are boilerplate.** A company coded for on-premises alcohol service may be
  a photography, events or holding business. The objective describes what was ticked at
  incorporation, not what the business does.
- **Unit-level succession is ordinary turnover.** 12% of units have hosted more than one company;
  the **median gap between consecutive registrations at a unit is 984 days (2.7 years)**. Two cases
  that looked like a "successor pattern" turned out to be *slower* than the street's own median —
  and one was not succession at all, since both companies were simultaneously active at the unit.
  Concurrent co-location occurs at ~4.8% of units and usually means related companies of one
  operator, or a shared registered address.

---

## 8. These businesses trade for real money while remaining invisible

**CONFIRMED by a documented sale listing.** A single-unit bar on the studied street was offered as a
going concern at **~THB 1.5m**, brokered by an agency, with the owner supplying the materials and a
refurbishment history described. **That venue matches no company in any register, in either script.**

For scale, a venue on an adjacent street was advertised at ~THB 2.5m some years earlier. Both are
**asking prices, not completions.**

**The implication:** the unaccounted-for majority are not dormant or notional premises. They are
operating businesses that change hands for millions of baht, entirely outside the company register,
the commercial register and VAT.

**Transfers do not move the paperwork.** What sells is the lease, the fittings and the goodwill. A
buyer registers afresh or not at all. **So a registration persisting in a former operator's name is
not evidence they still hold the venue** — registration status shows only that nobody cancelled it.

---

## 9. Enforcement is incident-generated, and this constrains every inference

**PRACTICE KNOWLEDGE from a local source, not independently verified — but it governs how the
evidence must be read.**

Permit and licensing rules here are **loosely enforced until something goes wrong**. Working on the
wrong visa class is cheaper than injecting another 2m of capital, so some operators take the risk —
especially unregistered venues, which cannot sponsor a permit at any price.

Three consequences, all easy to violate:

1. **A venue with no enforcement history is not compliant — it has not had an incident.** The null is
   uninformative. Quiet records must not be read as clean records.
2. **Venues visible in the legal record are selected for having had something go wrong**, which makes
   them systematically unrepresentative. Comparing "venues in court records" against "venues not in
   court records" is confounded by the incident, not by conduct.
3. **An incident exposes everything at once.** A raid will surface unpermitted staff as a secondary
   consequence, independent of whatever prompted it — so permit exposure clusters *around* an
   enforcement event without being evidence about that event's subject matter.

---

## 10. Why the sector *looks* opaque when mostly it isn't

Four administrative facts generate most of the apparent opacity:

- **Transliteration is lossy in both directions.** The same person is rendered several ways across
  government systems — and even within a single organisation's own systems. The Thai string is not a
  stable key either; one individual appeared under three spellings across three registers.
- **Surnames are family-unique by statute.** Registering a surname that duplicates an existing one is
  prohibited, with national enforcement since the early 1990s. **Two people sharing an uncommon Thai
  surname are, as a rule, one family** — the inverse of the Western prior, and it makes name sweeps
  far more informative than they would be elsewhere. (Caveat: uniqueness binds at registration and
  was district-level before digitisation, so toponymic surnames may have been registered
  independently in more than one province.)
- **English-language interfaces are reduced subsets** of the Thai ones, and carry their own date-
  conversion bugs. Anything read from an English interface needs checking against the Thai.
- **The registers are not one system.** Company data, director data, shareholder data, commercial
  registrations and filed accounts sit in different services with different access models, languages
  and paywalls. **Treating them as one system with one paywall produces repeated false conclusions
  about what is and isn't obtainable** — this was got wrong four separate times before the pattern
  was recognised.

## 11. Motivation, summarised

Stripped to essentials, the structures answer four questions and nothing else:

1. **"Do I need to put a foreigner on a work permit?"** — If no: register nothing, or take a sole
   proprietorship. If yes: incorporate a limited company and set capital at 2m per foreigner (1m if
   married to a Thai).
2. **"Is the foreigner married to a Thai?"** — If yes: the Thai spouse holds the company, the
   foreigner holds nothing and works on a permit. If no: the foreigner takes 49% openly and sits as
   a director.
3. **"Am I still paying for something I no longer need?"** — Permit-carrying companies file and
   survive; the rest are abandoned and struck off. Migration *down* the ladder — from company to sole
   proprietorship — is what it looks like when there is no longer a foreigner to sponsor.
4. **"Is it cheaper to comply or to risk it?"** — The comparison is explicit: another 2m of paid-in
   capital against the cost of a wrong-class visa. Loose enforcement makes the risk rational until an
   incident makes it catastrophic.

**Capital levels are compliance numbers, not business numbers. Companies exist to carry people, not
to trade. Most venues need neither, and their invisibility is the ordinary consequence of that.**

---

## 12. Methodological cautions earned the hard way

Recorded because each of these produced a false lead that survived until it was tested against the
population:

- **Before treating any feature as characteristic, ask how often it occurs by chance in the same
  population.** Capital of 4m, per-venue company structures, unit-level succession, shared
  addresses, co-registration dates and 100% Thai ownership all looked distinctive and all turned out
  to be the sector norm. The cheapest disproof is usually a sector-wide export.
- **For infrastructure markers, establish whether a value is assigned per *tenant* or per
  *platform*.** Platform values look like fingerprints and are not.
- **If a probability is quoted, the denominator must have a cited source.** Computing one from an
  invented parameter is worse than having no number, because a number carries authority a hunch does
  not.
- **An independent check must use a field the match did not already depend on.** Restating a match
  under a different heading is not corroboration.
- **Read the stated count on any registry page first.** Truncated parsing of a multi-entry page
  manufactured a striking and false cross-jurisdiction pattern.
- **When a search returns a mixed bag, check the context of every candidate before discarding any.**
  Pattern matching finds candidates; only context grades them.
- **When a subject's properties go dormant and get pruned, the archive is the primary source, not a
  fallback.** Several key records exist only as archived captures of pages now returning 404 or 500.
- **Social and directory sources reach what registers structurally cannot** — venue trading names,
  rebrands, named staff, direct contact numbers. A page URL preserving a pre-rebrand name is a
  dated, free succession record for premises that appear in no register anywhere.
